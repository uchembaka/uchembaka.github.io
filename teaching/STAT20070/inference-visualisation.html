Inference Visualisation

A bus comes every 10 minutes and you arrive at a random moment, so your wait is anywhere from 0 to 10 minutes, all equally likely. Average a few waits and the averages pile up into a bell curve. From there the lab builds up to inference: standardising, z versus t, a first look at testing and confidence intervals. It follows the in-class R script, inference_visualization, section by section, with nothing to install.

R script §1 · Single waiting time

One waiting time

Your wait Y is equally likely to be anything between 0 and 10 minutes: Y ~ Uniform(0, 10). Each draw is one simulated trip to the bus stop: the computer picks a random arrival time within the 10 minutes between buses and records how long you wait, which is what runif(1, 0, 10) does in R. Draw waits one at a time, then thousands, and watch the histogram flatten out. This flat shape is the population we will average from.

x_new <- runif(1, 0, 10) draws_n1 <- c(draws_n1, x_new)

What is a draw?

A draw is one random waiting time. Imagine arriving at the stop at a random moment: the time until the next bus is your wait. The computer imitates this by picking a number between 0 and 10 at random, with every value equally likely. In R this is runif(1, 0, 10).

“Draw 1 wait” does this once and adds one line to the strip. “Draw 10” does it ten times, giving ten separate waits.

R script §2 to 4 · Mean of n waiting times

Average n waits

Now wait for the bus n times and take the mean. Each sample gives one mean ȳ (tall bar); its n waits are the crosses. Repeat many times and look at the histogram of the means: it is centred on 5, gets narrower as n grows, and turns into a bell curve.

Waits per sample n 5
x <- runif(n, 0, 10); m <- mean(x) means <- replicate(N, mean(runif(n, 0, 10))) lines(x, dnorm(x, mean(means), sd(means)))

What is a sample here?

One sample is n draws, that is n separate waiting times. Its mean ȳ is a single number, shown as the tall line. “Draw one sample” takes one new sample of n waits and adds its mean to the strip.

R script §6 · Standardisation

Standardise the means

Subtract μ and divide by the standard error σ/√n. The histogram keeps its shape and only the x-axis changes: every sampling distribution becomes N(0, 1). That is why ±1.96 marks the middle 95% for any n.

Waits per sample n 100
std_err <- pop_std / sqrt(n) z <- (means - mu) / std_err q1 <- 5 - 1.96*std_err; q2 <- 5 + 1.96*std_err 100*mean(means > q1 & means < q2)

R script §7 · Non-uniform example

Start from a strange population

What if the population is lumpy or lopsided? Pick a shape, sample n values with replacement, and average. With n large enough the means still come out Normal: that is the "regardless of the original distribution" part of the CLT.

y <- c(rnorm(300000, 2.5, 1), rnorm(400000, 6.5, 0.75), rnorm(200000, 10, 0.5)) pop <- y[y > 0 & y < 10] replicate(N, mean(sample(pop, n, replace = TRUE)))

R script §8 · Known vs unknown population SD

σ known or estimated: z versus t

Population N(μ = 10, σ = 2). For each sample we standardise ȳ twice: with the true σ (z) and with the sample s (t). Because s is itself random, and often too small when n is small, t has heavier tails than N(0, 1). Student's t with n − 1 degrees of freedom describes it exactly.

M <- matrix(rnorm(N*n, 10, 2), N, n) m <- rowMeans(M); s <- apply(M, 1, sd) z <- (m - mu)/(sigma/sqrt(n)) t <- (m - mu)/(s/sqrt(n)) dt(x, df = n - 1)

Red: |z| or |t| above 3. Values like that are rare for z but common for t when n is small, usually because s came out small.

R script §9 · Sugar concentration example

Is this sample unusual if μ = 11?

Twelve sugar concentrations have mean 11.43. Suppose the true mean were 11. Simulate many samples of 12 from N(11, s²) and ask how often chance alone gives a sample mean at least as large as the one observed. This is the idea behind the hypothesis tests that come next.

sim <- matrix(rnorm(N*n, 11, sd(y)), N, n) m <- rowMeans(sim) t_stat <- (mean(y) - 11)/(sd(y)/sqrt(n)) mean(m > mean(y))

R script §9 · Confidence intervals

Do the intervals really cover μ?

Same N(10, 2²) population. Each simulated sample gives an interval, and since μ = 10 is known we can count how often it is inside. With σ known the z-interval works; with σ estimated, only the t-interval keeps its promised coverage for small n.
