Errors and Power

Every test can get it wrong in two ways. See where the two error probabilities come from, how they trade off, how a bigger sample shrinks both, how many subjects a study needs, and why “significant” does not always mean “important”.

1

Two ways to get it wrong

Think of a pregnancy test. H₀: not pregnant (no effect). HA: pregnant (an effect). The test says “positive” (reject H₀) or “negative” (do not reject), and either answer can be wrong.

αPr(Type I error): reject H₀ when it is true. We choose α in advance (0.10, 0.05, 0.01).
βPr(Type II error): do not reject H₀ when it is false. A real effect goes undetected.
Power = 1 − βPr(detect a real effect): reject H₀ when it is false. A drug that works is shown to work.

α is fixed by us. β and power depend on how big the effect is, how noisy the data are, and how many observations we have.

How many subjects do I need?

When planning a study, fix α, the power you want, the effect Δ worth detecting and an estimate of σ, then solve for n. Always round up.

4

Significant is not the same as important

A new anti-hypertensive drug against a reference drug, H₀: μ₁ = μ₂ vs HA: μ₁ > μ₂ (change in blood pressure). Same question, two very different studies.

With huge samples (high power) even a tiny difference becomes statistically significant; with small samples (low power) a large, clinically relevant difference can be missed. Always report the size of the effect next to the p-value.
